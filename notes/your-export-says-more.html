Somebody outside the company asks for "a sample of your data". A supplier quoting for a tool, a consultant, an AI chat that will write the formula for you. You export last month's orders and press send. That file carries more than anyone needed.

The second table is just as good for building a report, a sorting tool or a quote. Whoever reads it can see how your orders flow. They can't see who you sell to, what you charge or who rings whom about the side gate.

The trick is knowing what to swap and what to leave alone. Swap too little and your customer list walks out of the door. Swap too much and the file turns into noise nobody can build anything on.

Where an export ends up

Data leaving the building used to mean a lost laptop. Now it is mostly ordinary work. These are the usual moments:

An AI chat

Paste in an export and ask for a formula, a summary or a chart. Depending on the service, its plan and its settings, what you paste can be kept and even used to train it.

A supplier or consultant

Anyone quoting for software, a report or a new system wants real-looking data first. The file then sits in their inbox and on their laptop for good.

A trial of a new system

ERP and planning vendors love a demo "on your own data". Their demo server is not your server.

A forum or a friend who knows Excel

The quickest way to get help with a spreadsheet is to share it. The file is then forwarded, saved and forgotten somewhere you can't see.

None of these is wrong. In each case the other side needs the shape of your data: which columns, what kind of values, how many rows, how they link. Almost never the real names.

Worth knowing

Names, emails and phone numbers of people are personal data under UK GDPR, even when the person works for a customer. Your customer list and your prices are not personal data, but they are often the most valuable thing in the file. This is not legal advice. It is the reason the habit is worth having.

What gives you away

Five kinds of column carry almost all the risk. If a column tells someone who or how much, swap it.

Swap

People

Contact names, emails, phone numbers, and the columns nobody thinks of: entered by, approved by, operator, driver, sales rep.

Contact · Email · EntryPerson · Rep

Swap

Customers and suppliers

Company names, delivery addresses, account numbers. Together they are your customer list, the thing a competitor would most like to see.

Customer · ShipToAddress · Account No

Swap

Money

Prices, costs, margins, discounts, wages. Scale them by one hidden factor instead of deleting them, so cheap stays cheap and dear stays dear.

Unit Price · Order Total · Cost

Empty

Free text

Notes, comments and delivery instructions. Names and phone numbers hide here, and a stand-in can't fix a sentence. Empty them unless the tool must read them.

Notes · Comments · Instructions

Check

Things you can't see in the grid

The file's author and company in its properties, cell comments, hidden sheets and columns, sheet names, and the file name itself. "Acme orders October.xlsx" says it all.

File › Info · hidden sheets · file name

What to leave alone

Everything that tells someone what and when usually stays as it is. That is the part a tool actually works with.

Keep

Quantities and counts

Rows, units, pieces, weights, run times. They say nothing about who you are and everything about how the work flows.

Qty · Pieces · Weight · Minutes

Keep

Your own codes

Part numbers, machine names, routes, statuses and courier names. Sorting rules and reports often depend on them. Swap them only if a code contains a customer's name.

Part · Machine · Status · Ship Via

Keep or shift

Dates and times

Dates drive almost every sort, report and plan. Keep them, or move them all back by the same whole number of weeks, so a Monday stays a Monday.

Order Date · Due · Start · End

Keep the link

The same thing, the same stand-in

If Acme becomes Customer 001 in one row and Customer 017 in the next, totals per customer vanish. Every swap must give the same value the same stand-in, in every file.

Acme → Customer 001, everywhere

The rule of thumb

Swap who and how much. Keep what and when. And never break the link between rows.

Check your own export in five minutes

Take the last file you sent outside the company, or the next one you are about to. No IT needed, just Excel and a pen.

  1. Mark the who and how much columns. Go along the header row and tick every column that names a person, a customer, a supplier or an amount of money. Most exports have more than people expect.
  2. Read ten notes. Filter the notes or comments column to non-blanks and read ten of them. Count the names and phone numbers.
  3. Look behind the grid. In Excel, File › Info shows the author and company. Right-click a sheet tab and choose Unhide to see hidden sheets. Hidden columns show as a jump in the letters along the top.
  4. Read the file name and sheet names as a stranger would.
  5. Ask what the other side needs. If they are building a tool or a report, they need the columns it reads, real-looking values and enough rows. That is the list of what to keep. Everything else can be swapped, emptied or removed.

What the tool does with the sample

  1. Groups the sheets of one job using Job No. Any stand-in with the same pattern works.
  2. Adds a separator with the sheet count from Sheets, so multi-page orders stay together.
  3. Stamps the courier from Ship Via and prints a preview of each Part.
  4. Sorts the day by Due and drafts a short priority note for the shift.

Customer and Entered by are never read, so they are swapped. Customer 001 still shows which jobs belong together. Notes go: the tool doesn't read them.

What the tool does with the sample

  1. Sorts the pile the way you choose: by Priority, by Value or by size from Qty.

Value is scaled by one hidden factor, so sorting by value still gives the same order. Postcode is swapped, keeping the first characters only if the tool sorts by area.
