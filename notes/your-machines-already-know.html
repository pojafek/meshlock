Your machines already know how long they stand still

Most factories already record how long every machine runs and stands still. Nobody reads it, because it sits inside the machine in a format built for service engineers.

I saw this first-hand in a UK factory running six machines. Everyone had a feel for how busy the machines were. "Pretty much flat out" was the usual answer. Nobody had a number, because nobody believed the data existed.

It did. Every machine kept its own job history. It just looked like noise: codes, timestamps, file paths, no totals. Once those files were turned into one readable view, the first measured day showed the machines running about 80% of planned time. A good chunk of every shift had gone missing between jobs, and no one had noticed.

The number alone changed things. Once the team could see it every day, the average climbed to about 91%. Same machines, same people. The only new thing was being able to see.

This guide shows you where that data hides on your own floor, who to ask, and what to do in your first afternoon.

Why the number in your head is too kind

If you track stoppages at all, you probably do it by hand. A sheet by the machine, a box on the job card, a figure at the end of the shift. That is honest work, but it misses the same things in every factory.

  • Short stops. Two minutes waiting for material does not feel worth writing down. Forty of them is over an hour.
  • The gap between jobs. Unloading, finding the next file, a quick chat. The machine is idle, but nobody calls it a stop.
  • Rounded memory. A stop written down at 10 pm about something at 3 pm becomes "about 15 minutes".
  • Running slow. A machine running at half speed counts as running. The lost time never shows up as a stop.

None of this is anyone's fault. People are there to make parts, not to keep a stopwatch. Companies that sell monitoring systems often say the first automatic measurement shows two to three times more lost time than the plant believed. Treat that as a rough guide, not a promise. The point is simpler: the machine keeps a more honest record than any of us can.

Who to ask

You do not need IT or a consultant to start. The answers usually sit with four people you already know.

The operator

Uses the screen every day and has seen menus nobody else opens.

"Is there a history, log or report screen on the machine? Can it save to a USB stick?"

Maintenance

Knows the drives, panels and hour counters inside the cabinet.

"Do the drives or the panel keep run hours or an alarm history? Can we read it without changing anything?"

The machine supplier's service engineer

Knows exactly what the controller stores and how to get it out.

"What does this controller log, how many days does it keep, and how do we export it?"

Whoever looks after the ERP

Knows what gets booked against each job.

"Do we record job start and finish times, or quantities per shift? Can I get last month as a spreadsheet?"

One tip: ask about exports, not changes. "Can we save what is already there?" gets a yes far more often than "Can we connect something to the machine?"

Six places the data hides

Start with the one closest to hand. You only need one source to begin.

1

The machine's own controller

A history of programs or jobs run, with start and finish times. Moulding machines often keep shot counts and cycle times.

Look for History, Log, Job report or Statistics on the screen. Many save as CSV to a USB stick.

When the machine actually worked, job by job.

2

The drive cabinet

Many drives count "run hours" and "power-on hours" separately.

Ask maintenance to read both from the drive's display. It needs no setting changed, but the cabinet is theirs: never open it yourself.

Two numbers, one ratio: how much of its switched-on time the machine really works.

3

The operator panel

Alarm history: every fault, when it started and when it cleared.

Often an "export alarm history" option to USB or SD card.

Which stops repeat, and how long each one costs.

4

Your ERP or job system

Job start and finish bookings, quantities per shift, scrap booked.

A standard report exported to Excel. Ask for one month.

Planned against actual, per job. Useful even with no machine data at all.

5

Your file folders

Programs and job files, each with the date and time it was saved.

Sort the folder by "date modified".

A rough timeline of what was prepared and sent to the floor.

6

The power cable

Nothing yet, on older machines with no screen worth asking.

A clamp-on current sensor on one wire of the supply. It does not touch the machine's controls, but it goes inside the electrical panel, so an electrician fits it.

Running, idle or off, minute by minute. On presses and moulding machines it can even count cycles.

For the curious

A controller is the small computer that runs the machine. It logs events for its own use and for service visits. Nobody designed those logs for a production manager, which is why they look so unfriendly.

One warning: most machines only keep a short history. Some keep about a month of jobs, some the last thousand cycles, some only the last day. Older records are written over.

Your first afternoon: save it, don't read it

Your only job today is to make sure the data stops disappearing. Understanding it can wait.

  1. Pick one machine. The busiest one, or the one everyone argues about.
  2. Look at its screen with the operator. Find the history, counter or alarm page.
  3. Write down the counters at the start and end of every shift for a week, and every stop over 5 minutes. The shift log in the checklist below is made for this.
  4. If the screen offers an export, ask whoever looks after the machine whether a USB stick is allowed. Many sites block them, and that is fine: a week of readings is enough to start. If you can export, save one file a week with the machine name and date in the file name, so the history stops being written over.

Then open the file, if you are curious. It will probably look something like this:

2026-10-06 06:02:11;EV17;0;D:\PRG\A4471_R2.PRG;S;0001
2026-10-06 06:02:14;EV03;1;;;
2026-10-06 06:41:57;EV18;0;D:\PRG\A4471_R2.PRG;E;0001;002385
2026-10-06 06:58:30;AL214;2;DOOR INTLK;;
2026-10-06 07:03:02;AL214;0;;;
2026-10-06 07:03:09;EV17;0;D:\PRG\A4471_R2.PRG;S;0002

If that means nothing to you, good. It means nothing to most people. It was written for a service engineer with the manual open, not for someone running a shift.

But look closer. Every line has a time. Somewhere in those lines is when the machine started, when it stopped, why it stopped, and for how long. Across a month of files, that is every shift you ran. You were just never given a way to read it. So let's read one.
